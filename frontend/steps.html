Anatomy of the wildfire

The anatomy

of a wildfire

Seven moments, from the quiet before to the green that returns. Scroll slowly: the page changes as the fire does.

01 — BEFORE

"A Forest is never just Trees"

Roots hold the soil. Leaves hold the shade. Thousands of species hold each other up. A forest stores carbon, cools the air and shelters a huge share of life on land. From the outside it looks still. Inside, it never stops.

Today, the forest covers about 31% of the globe’s surface, for a total area of 4.06 billion hectares, or more than 40,000,000 km². More than half of this area is located in six countries: Russia, Brazil, Canada, the United States, China, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

02 — CONDITIONS

“Nothing Happens Yet. That’s the Problem”

A dry season runs a little longer. Temperatures sit a few degrees above normal. Leaves, needles and fallen branches lose their moisture and turn into fuel. No alarm sounds, and from the outside nothing has changed. But the conditions are lining up, quietly, one after another.

18.7% is how much the fire weather season, the stretch of the year when heat, low humidity, rain-free days and wind make wildfires likely to spread, lengthened on average across the world between 1979 and 2013. It grew longer over about a quarter of the planet’s vegetated land, some 29.6 million km², and the area hit by unusually long fire weather seasons more than doubled.

03 — IGNITION

“It Only Takes One Spark”

Sometimes it’s lightning on dry ground. Depending on where you are, it’s often us: a discarded cigarette, a sparking machine, an unattended campfire, a deliberate act. The origin changes from one continent to the next. The first minutes look the same everywhere: a small flame, and a forest that is ready for it.

Worldwide, an estimated 85 to 95% of wildfires are started by people, but the balance shifts from one region to the next. About 97% of fires with a known cause in Europe are linked to human activity. In the intact forests outside the tropics, lightning dominates instead: lightning-ignited fires account for 77% of the area burned.

04 — POINT OF NO RETURN

“Then the fire starts making its own rules”

Wind pushes the flames toward fresh fuel. Heat dries the vegetation ahead of the front. Embers lift off and land far away, starting new fires beyond the line firefighters are trying to hold. A fire that crawled along the ground reaches the treetops, and from there it moves faster than anyone can follow on foot.

In just 90 minutes on the afternoon of 8 November 2018, California’s Camp Fire grew by about 4,000 hectares (10,000 acres), roughly three-quarters of a hectare every second. The fire had started that morning in the hills of northern California, and it reached the town of Paradise within about two hours. In its first 24 hours, it burned some 28,000 hectares.

05 — SPREAD

“From above, you can watch it grow.”

Satellites pass over and register heat: a point, then dozens, then thousands. Each one marks a place where fire was detected. Day after day the cluster widens while the green around it shrinks. It’s a scale that’s hard to feel from the ground, and the one the data makes visible.

At least 370 million hectares burned worldwide between March 2024 and February 2025, an area larger than India. That was about 9% below the long-term average, which makes it a quieter year than most. About 70% of that burned area was in tropical grasslands, savannahs and shrublands rather than in forests.

06 — WHAT'S LEFT

“The flames end. The damage doesn’t.”

Burned soil can repel water, so the first heavy rain brings erosion and floods instead of relief. Smoke travels hundreds of kilometers. Carbon that took decades to store returns to the air in days. Animals that couldn’t flee lose their habitat, and people lose far more than scenery.

In 2023, wildfires realeased about 2,700Mt of carbon worldwide, roughly 7.7 billion tonnes of CO₂. Canada’s record season alone accounted for 23% of that: about 480 megatonnes of carbon, almost five times its average of the previous twenty years.

07 — REGROWTH

“And yet, something comes back.”

Sometimes within weeks, green shoots break through the ash. Some trees resprout from their bark; some pines only release their seeds under heat. Fire is part of how certain ecosystems renew themselves. But renewal has limits: when fires become too frequent or too intense, a forest may not return as it was, or at all.

25.6% of the forests hit by large wildfires since 2010 have seen their recovery stall, up from 22.6% before. Across 3,281 large fires worldwide between 2001 and 2021, fewer than a third of forests grew back within seven years. The more severe the fire, the slower the return, especially in boreal forests.
